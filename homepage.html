Social Engineering & the Human Factor

Social engineering attacks the person rather than the system. These are the areas I study and write about, and the lens I bring to every security review.

  • Phishing & spoofed sites

    Why users and even developers trust a site that merely looks legitimate, and how attackers abuse visual trust signals.

  • Pretexting & impersonation

    How attackers build believable stories to obtain access, credentials or information, and the process controls that stop them.

  • Insider threats

    The hardest vulnerability to deter: people with legitimate privileges. This is the motivation behind my research (see Research).

  • Awareness & reconnaissance

    OSINT tools such as Recon-ng and SpiderFoot show how much about a person or organisation is publicly exposed, and why awareness training matters.

Case Study: The Padlock Myth

“The Padlock Myth: Why HTTPS = Safe Is Dangerously Wrong.” The padlock only tells you the connection is encrypted, not that the site is trustworthy. Phishing sites can and do use valid certificates.

As a software developer who builds web applications, I tend to trust HTTPS, which is exactly why this myth matters to me: a technical safeguard can become a false sense of security.

Research

Postgraduate Diploma in Cybersecurity · Nelson Mandela University

Adoption of distributed ledger technology (DLT) for immutable, tamper-evident storage of banking data, combined with machine-learning anomaly detection

  • Type: Literature-based technical research (systematic literature review)
  • Corpus: 20 peer-reviewed papers
  • Sources searched: IEEE Xplore and ScienceDirect
  • Status: Report in progress (2026)

Why this topic

During my cybersecurity internship I worked on the defensive side of security and concluded that the human vulnerability, the insider threat, is the hardest to deter. My database programming background showed me why: in Microsoft SQL Server, a privileged user can edit a table directly, and in most cases there may be no trace that the data was changed by hand. For banking data, that is a serious integrity risk.

Research question

How can a permissioned distributed ledger such as Hyperledger Fabric, together with machine-learning based anomaly detection, make unauthorised changes to banking records harder to hide and easier to detect?

Key concepts

  • Permissioned ledger

    Hyperledger Fabric as a ledger where known participants record transactions in an append-only, tamper-evident way.

  • Anomaly detection

    Machine-learning methods that flag unusual activity, such as insider misuse, in activity logs and records.

  • Insider threat

    Risk from privileged users who can alter data directly, the central problem the review is organised around.

  • Defence in depth

    Layered controls and separation of duties, with the ledger and detection acting as complementary layers.

Method

  1. Define the insider-threat inclusion criteria and search terms.
  2. Search IEEE Xplore and ScienceDirect, then screen titles, abstracts and full texts.
  3. Assess the quality of each included paper and group findings by concept.
  4. Synthesise the evidence into a layered framework and discuss limitations and South African banking context.

This is a literature-based study: it reviews existing evidence and proposes a conceptual framework, and it does not report a built or deployed system.
